---
title: I've Grown Weary of Lifemaxxing
description:
  We often spend our time an energy on activities that supposedly improve our
  lives, but if we are out of touch with who we are as individuals, does any of
  that activity even mean anything to our life stories?
published: 2026-11-22T17:00:00+0000
tags: ['Skills: Soft']
---

I was toiling away in my "yard" yesterday, pulling weeds and cleaning up
detritus that has landed on our property from the windy climate that we choose
to subject ourselves to. (I put "yard" in quotes because here in El Paso, grass
doesn't thrive, meaning most home landscapes are primarily a blanket of gravel
and astro-turf. I use a leaf-blower as a lawnmower, but I've even heard some
people say they use a vacuum outside to keep their turf looking nice. Seems like
a bad ROI to destroy a vacuum's electric motor in pursuit of a clean lawn to me,
but I digress.) As I began this work, I realized that I felt disconnected from
the task my body was performing, due to a sense or feeling that "I should be
doing something more important, more 'productive' than this." That creeping
synthetic sense of urgency seems to pervade much of my life, but I can't quite
put my finger on why that is. What _would_ be more productive? What other task
_should_ I be spending my time on?

I think a lot of this stems from how we as people tend to find meaning in our
lives, and what we tend to think of as valuable. In an increasingly
capitalistically controlled society where enshittifiers get to extract as much
economic value from laborers as possible, there's a giant disconnect between
"financial value" and "human value." I fall into the trap all the time, of
telling myself an activity I enjoy that provides no benefit to my family in a
financial sense is a waste of my time, and even worse, is selfish. Thoughts that
often destroy my will to get started on leisurely activities include:

- It's folly to deeply engage in making music because I can't sell it.
- It's irresponsible to spend time writing in my blog if I can't monetize it.
- it's vice to spend time getting better at Super Smash Brothers to keep up with
  my kids when we play together.

Clearly I've spruced these up to make them sound more flowery in prose than how
they actually arrive in my little cranium, but you get the gist. In summary:

> "Any activity not aimed at furthering my financial standing (capital
> ownership) or career opportunities (the means of increasing my capital
> ownership) is a sinful waste of my time."

I _know_ deep down that this is not true. It would be a hellish life to live if
we were to never spend any of it doing things that we enjoy for joy's sake. In
C.S. Lewis' _Screwtape Letters_, one of the lies from the demon is "..." Whether
or not you believe in demons per se, there is quite a bit of truth to this
sneaky little lie that we tend to believe. As an insufferable hipster doofus
millenial, I'm well versed in so many life hacks and so-called optimizations to
live a "better" life, that it's hard to tease apart sometimes whether I do
something because I actually believe it makes my life better, or if it's just
some technique I picked up along the way that no longer serves me well
personally. On the flip side, maybe sometimes doing things the better way really
is just, better. Maybe being more efficient or pragmatic _does_ free us up to
spend more time and energy on the people or activities we _do_ love and care
about.

Case in point, I try to go to the gym regularly, because exercise is
subjectively a good thing for human beings to engage in, given our increasingly
digital lives and worlds that do not require physical engagement in the way that
we evolved from. _But_ I am so used to looking for optimizations and
maximizations that I often question "_Why_ am I so focused on building muscle
and strength when I don't care about being big or strong?" It's mostly because
the most efficient way of keeping lean is to have more muscle mass, and I'd
rather not have to spend hours a day running to be able to burn off all the
calories my fat inner child desires.

I don't have a good answer for where the ideal trade-off lies, but that's one of
the best examples I have of an activity I regularly engage in with a "subjective
ideal" in mind instead of just saying "Screw it, I _like_ to run and throw a
kettlebell around more than I like to load up a barbell with as much weight as
my body can handle." That kind of mindset can carry over into other activities
too, like making art (I have to learn and use proper techniques to be the _best_
artist, not just someone who can express myself) or writing code (I _must_ use
the latest tools to make as big of an impact as fast I possibly can, instead of
taking time to understand what the hell I'm actually building and how it works),
or writing in general (I need for the things I write to be read by an
increasingly larger audience or I'm failing at it).

I suppose this is where the advent of prolific AI tooling is a double edged
sword. If we leverage it in a way that just feeds the capitalistic machine, and
are constantly in search of "maximum impact," and generating as much output as
individuals as we possibly can, I don't think that _we_ benefit so much as the
"winners" of capitalism do. Falling into the trap of perpetuating the rat race
and being content with the little piece of cheese they so graciously provide
doesn't do anything but enrich the purveyors of said rat race. This isn't to say
that I feel my whole life is a scam, it's fun to have modern gadgets and
niceties as a reward for my labor, but I am very skeptical that anyone at "the
top" is still concerned with making life _better_ for middle to low income
people, or humanity in general. There's just no way that we have the amount of
information we do at this point in human history and _don't_ have a means of
ensuring everyone has basic necessities for life like food, clean water, and
shelter.

However, I think that if we leverage these tools in a way that augments our
curiosity, that helps us dig deeper into things that we care about and want to
understand for ourselves, that we can actually _improve_ our humanity rather
than degrade it by offloading our cognitive load to the robot. We can become
more helpful to those around us by enhancing our depth of knowledge. We can drum
up solutions to the problems of poverty, division and self-destruction that
balance the playing I believe that we owe it to each other to set the example
that we aren't the selfish creatures these hyper-capitalist "winners" say people
are meant to be. There's enough food to go around. There's enough housing.
There's no reason that we can't find a way to share it, other than that the
people who claim ownership are greedy for more ownership. Laws and governing
entities are figments of our collective imagination, are we really so dull and
lifeless that we can't imagine something better at this point?
